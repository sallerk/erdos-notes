A theorem ruling out the symmetric families, and an exact reproduction of Danzer's construction.

No counterexample. What follows is one theorem, one structural reason the $k = 4$ case is harder than $k = 3$, and an exact $9$-point artifact. For context on what is already spent: AlphaEvolve attacked this problem numerically (Problem 6.53 of "Mathematical exploration and discovery at scale", arXiv:2511.02864), scoring vertices by equidistance error, and reached $k = 3$ but not $k = 4$. Everything below is on the exact and symmetric side, which that approach does not reach.

How this sits with the work already on this page. Two threads here bound the size of a counterexample. TheAbandonedThinker's counting argument gives $n \ge 7$, with equality forcing a $2$-$(7,4,2)$ design. mysticflounder's Lean formalization goes further: as of its current state, both the counting engine giving $n \ge 9$ and the $n = 9$ base case are unconditionally proved and kernel-checked, and what remains open there is the descent step — that a counterexample on more than $9$ points contains a removable vertex.

Everything below is orthogonal to both. It restricts symmetry rather than size, and it holds for every $n$, so it bears on the part of that programme that is still open rather than on the part already closed. Nothing below contradicts either thread.

One circle is never enough. If all points lie on a circle of radius $R$ about $O$ then $|v_\alpha - v_\beta| = 2R|\sin((\alpha-\beta)/2)|$, so two vertices are equidistant from $v$ exactly when they are symmetric about the diameter through $v$. Every distance from a vertex of a cyclic polygon therefore has multiplicity at most $2$, and even $k = 3$ needs at least two radii.

THEOREM. Suppose a counterexample has dihedral symmetry $D_m$ with every vertex on a mirror line. A line through the centre meets a convex curve twice, so each of the $m$ mirrors carries at most $2$ vertices and $n \le 2m$; mirror orbits have size $m$, so $n = m$ or $n = 2m$. The case $n = m$ is a regular $m$-gon, dead by the paragraph above. The case $n = 2m$ forces two orbits whose phases differ by $\pi/m$, giving the alternating $2m$-gon with vertices $v_l = \rho_l e^{i \pi l / m}$ for $l = 0, \dots, 2m-1$, where $\rho_l = 1$ when $l$ is even and $\rho_l = b$ when $l$ is odd. This is in convex position exactly when $\cos(\pi/m) < b < 1/\cos(\pi/m)$. For every $m \ge 2$ and every $b$ in that window, the distances $|v_0 - v_l|$ for $l = 0, \dots, m-1$ are strictly increasing. Hence each distance from $v_0$ is attained at most twice among $v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}$ and their mirror images, plus at most once by the antipodal $v_m$: the maximum equidistant count is at most $3$, never $4$.

Proof sketch. Write $c_i = \cos(i\pi/m)$. For $l$ even the step needs $b^2 - 2c_{l+1}b + 2c_l - 1 > 0$ on $b > \cos(\pi/m)$; this is an upward parabola whose larger root lies below $\cos(\pi/m)$ precisely when $(c_1 - c_{l+1})^2 - (c_{l+1}^2 - 2c_l + 1) > 0$, and that expression is identically $2\sin((l+1)\pi/m)\sin(\pi/m) - \sin^2(\pi/m)$, positive because $\sin((l+1)\pi/m) \ge \sin(\pi/m)$ for $1 \le l+1 \le m-1$. For $l$ odd the corresponding quantity is $\sin(\pi/m)(2\sin((l+1)\pi/m)\cos(\pi/m) - \sin(\pi/m))$, positive for $l \le m-2$. Both identities check symbolically. The $l = m$ step is genuinely different — there the sequence can drop — and that is exactly the loophole that lets multiplicity $3$ exist while $4$ does not.

COROLLARY. No counterexample to this problem has dihedral symmetry $D_m$, $m \ge 2$, with all of its vertices on mirror lines.

Why $k = 3$ is easy and $k = 4$ is hard. A configuration symmetric under a finite group of plane isometries gives a vertex $v$ equidistant partners for free from two sources: the stabiliser of $v$, and $v$'s own orbit. Either way a vertex of a symmetric convex configuration has at most $2$ other vertices equidistant from it for free. So $k = 3$ costs one equation per orbit and $k = 4$ costs two. Counting parameters against equations, a $C_m$ configuration with $c$ orbits has $2c-2$ free parameters against $c$ equations for $k = 3$ and $2c$ for $k = 4$; a $D_m$ configuration with $a$ mirror orbits and $g$ generic ones has $a + 2g - 1$ parameters against $a+g$ and $a+2g$. For $k = 3$ under $C_3$ the system is underdetermined as soon as $c \ge 3$ — which is exactly Danzer's nine points, three equations in four unknowns, a one-parameter family. For $k = 4$ every symmetric family is overdetermined by at least $1$.

The cheap escape is also blocked. If two orbits of a $C_m$-symmetric convex polygon are phase-aligned, their union is an alternating $2m$-gon in convex position, so by the theorem above the distance from a vertex to the aligned pair never equals the distance to its own orbit pair. Hence a $C_m$-symmetric counterexample needs at least two equations per orbit.

The least overdetermined surviving family is $D_m$ with one mirror orbit and one generic orbit $\rho e^{i(\pm\theta + 2\pi k/m)}$, $0 < \theta < \pi/m$, so $n = 3m$. Its generic vertex already has its two same-chirality orbit mates equidistant and needs two more at that distance. Two of the three possible sources die for every $m$: both extras from the mirror orbit forces $2\theta = 2\pi(k_1+k_2)/m$, putting the vertex on a mirror; both from the opposite-chirality half forces $\theta = \pi/(2m)$ and then $\sin(\pi/(2m)) = \sin(\pi j/m)$, needing $j = 1/2$ or $m - 1/2$, never an integer. So one extra must come from each source, leaving three equations in the two unknowns $(\rho, \theta)$ — overdetermined by exactly one, with only finitely many index choices. A dense grid over $m = 3, \dots, 8$ found no solution anywhere in the convex region, the closest being a worst-vertex spread of about $1.2 \times 10^{-2}$ at $m = 5$. Turning that last step into an exact resultant over $\mathbb{Q}(e^{2\pi i/m})$ is small — two unknowns, degrees at most $4$ — and would upgrade this from "a grid found nothing" to "no counterexample of this shape exists, for every $m$". I did not run it, and it is the concrete piece I would pick up next.

A conditional remark on the common-distance version. If all the witness radii are equal, each edge of the witness graph is one equation serving two vertices, so the unknowns number $2n-3$ against $|E| \ge kn/2$ equations. For $k = 3$ that is $1.5n < 2n-3$, which holds from $n \ge 7$ onwards (at $n = 6$ both sides equal $9$), so the system is underdetermined — which is why Fishburn and Reeds could find their $20$-point example. For $k = 4$ it needs $2n$ unit distances among $n$ points in convex position. The best known lower bound there is Edelsbrunner–Hajnal's $2n-7$, believed tight, with only Füredi's $O(n \log n)$ proved above it. So the common-distance $k = 4$ version is impossible if the $2n-7$ bound is tight — conditional on that, which I did not attempt.

Finally, an artifact others may find useful. Danzer's theorem is usually cited without coordinates, so here is a constructive version: a $C_3$-symmetric convex $9$-gon, each orbit an equilateral triangle, which gives two equidistant vertices free and leaves three equations in four unknowns. One member has six of its vertices at $(1,0)$, $(-1/2, \sqrt{3}/2)$, $(-1/2, -\sqrt{3}/2)$, $(-1/2+\sqrt{3},\ \sqrt{3}/2)$, $(-\sqrt{3}/2-1/2,\ 3/2-\sqrt{3}/2)$ and $(1-\sqrt{3}/2,\ -3/2)$, with the remaining three in a field of degree at most $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. All nine vertices have maximum equidistant count exactly $3$, verified in exact arithmetic with no floating point, and the polygon is in convex position. Every vertex is exactly one short of $k = 4$. Full coordinates available on request.

Everything above marked as a theorem was checked symbolically and cross-checked numerically at 50 digits for $m$ up to $60$, and by an independent screen to $m = 200$; that screen initially disagreed, and the theorem is what exposed the bug in it (catastrophic cancellation in the quadratic formula when $6 \mid m$).
